### Benn's Transformation

Benn is an intelligent individual who graduated from the University of Virginia with a degree in economics. Like many graduates, he had career ambitions, but he soon realized that his skills were limited to an Excel macro. To enhance his marketability, Benn decided to leave his job as a "human spreadsheet" and pursue a career in computer programming, despite having no coding experience.

Benn didn’t have that luxury. After resigning from his finance job, he returned home to prepare for his next move. His parents were supportive but concerned about the length of his stay. Benn needed to acquire a hard skill quickly.

This is where he faced a common hurdle: the ability to engage in deep work. Learning programming requires intense concentration on complex concepts, a focus that many knowledge workers, including Benn, struggle to maintain. Reflecting on his previous mindset, Benn admitted, "I was constantly on the Internet, checking my email; it was a compulsion I couldn't resist." He shared a story about a project where he failed to focus, spending "98 percent of my time" browsing the Web instead of completing a business plan.

Benn quickly secured a developer position at a San Francisco tech start-up, earning $100,000—an amount that could grow significantly in Silicon Valley. When I last spoke with him, he was thriving, having transformed his work habits to prioritize focus.

Benn's journey illustrates that deep work is not just a nostalgic notion; it is a vital skill in today's information economy. As technology evolves rapidly, mastering the ability to learn complex concepts quickly is essential. Deep work is crucial for anyone seeking to advance in a competitive landscape, where the best work is rewarded.

Deep Work Is Valuable

Less than a year later, ESPN and ABC News lured Silver away from the Times, positioning him to influence various media segments.

What accounts for the success of Silver, Hansson, and Doerr? There are two types of answers to this question. The first focuses on the micro-level, examining the personality traits and strategies that propelled this trio’s rise. The second type takes a macro perspective, concentrating less on the individuals and more on the nature of the work they represent. While both approaches are important, the macro answers are particularly relevant to our discussion, as they shed light on what our current economy rewards.

To delve into this macro perspective, I turn to the insights of MIT economists Erik Brynjolfsson and Andrew McAfee. In their influential 2011 book, *Race Against the Machine*, they argue that the rise of digital technology is transforming our labor markets in unexpected ways. “We are in the early throes of a Great Restructuring,” Brynjolfsson and McAfee explain. “Our technologies are racing ahead, but many of our skills and organizations are lagging behind.” For many workers, this lag spells trouble. As intelligent machines advance and the gap between machine and human capabilities narrows, employers are increasingly inclined to hire “new machines” instead of “new people.” When only a human will suffice, advancements in communication and collaboration technology are making remote work easier than ever, prompting companies to outsource key roles to top talent—leaving local job seekers underemployed.

However, this reality is not entirely bleak. Brynjolfsson and McAfee emphasize that the Great Restructuring does not uniformly diminish all jobs; rather, it divides them. While many individuals may lose out in this new economy as their skills become automatable or easily outsourced, others will not only survive but thrive, becoming more valued—and thus more rewarded—than ever before. Brynjolfsson and McAfee are not alone in proposing this bimodal trajectory for the economy. In 2013, George Mason economist Tyler Cowen published *Average Is Over*, echoing this thesis of a digital divide. What makes Brynjolfsson and McAfee’s analysis particularly valuable is their identification of three specific groups that will prosper in this new landscape and reap a disproportionate share of the benefits of the Intelligent Machine Age. Unsurprisingly, Silver, Hansson, and Doerr belong to these three groups. Let’s examine each of these groups to understand why they are suddenly so valuable.

### The High-Skilled Workers

Brynjolfsson and McAfee refer to the group represented by Nate Silver as “high-skilled” workers. While advancements such as robotics and voice recognition are automating many low-skilled positions, these economists emphasize that “other technologies like data visualization, analytics, high-speed communications, and rapid prototyping have augmented the contributions of more abstract and data-driven reasoning, increasing the value of these jobs.” Those who can work with and extract valuable insights from increasingly complex machines will thrive. Tyler Cowen bluntly summarizes this reality: “The key question will be: are you good at working with intelligent machines or not?”

Nate Silver exemplifies the high-skilled worker, as his success hinges on his ability to engage with complex technology. Intelligent machines are not obstacles to Silver’s success; rather, they are prerequisites for it.

### The Superstars

David Heinemeier Hansson illustrates the second group that Brynjolfsson and McAfee predict will flourish in our new economy: the “superstars.” High-speed data networks and collaboration tools, such as email and virtual meeting software, have dismantled regional barriers in many sectors of knowledge work. It no longer makes sense to hire a full-time programmer, allocate office space, and provide benefits when you can instead pay one of the world’s top programmers, like Hansson, just enough to complete a project. This arrangement typically yields better results for less money, while allowing Hansson to serve many more clients each year.

The fact that Hansson might be working remotely from Marbella, Spain, while your office is in Des Moines, Iowa, is inconsequential to your company, as advancements in communication and collaboration technology make the process nearly seamless. However, this reality does impact less-skilled local programmers in Des Moines who rely on a steady paycheck. This trend extends to an increasing number of fields where technology enables productive remote work—consulting, marketing, writing, design, and more. Once the talent market becomes universally accessible, those at the top thrive while the rest struggle.

In a seminal 1981 paper, economist Sherwin Rosen explored the mathematics behind these “winner-take-all” markets. One of his key insights was to model talent as a factor with “imperfect substitution.” Rosen explained, “Hearing a succession of mediocre singers does not add up to a single outstanding performance.” In other words, talent cannot be aggregated like a commodity; there is a premium for being the best. Consequently, in a marketplace where consumers have access to all performers and can accurately assess their talent, they will choose the very best. Even a small talent advantage can lead superstars to capture the majority of the market.

### The Owners

The final group poised to thrive in our new economy—epitomized by John Doerr—consists of those with capital to invest in the new technologies driving the Great Restructuring. Access to capital provides significant advantages, particularly during favorable economic periods. Brynjolfsson and McAfee point out that postwar Europe was a poor time to hold onto cash, as rapid inflation and aggressive taxation swiftly eroded old fortunes.

In contrast, the Great Restructuring presents a favorable environment for those with capital. As digital technology reduces the need for labor in many industries, the share of rewards allocated to those who own intelligent machines is increasing. A venture capitalist today can fund a company like Instagram, which was ultimately sold for a billion dollars, while employing only thirteen people. When has there ever been a time in history where such a small labor input could yield such immense value? With minimal labor involvement, the proportion of wealth flowing back to machine owners is unprecedented.

Current economic thought suggests that the unprecedented growth and impact of technology are instigating a massive restructuring of our economy. In this new landscape, three groups will hold a distinct advantage: those who can work effectively and creatively with intelligent machines, those who excel in their fields, and those with access to capital.

The pressing question we must now confront is: How does one join these winners? While I have no secret formula for quickly amassing capital and becoming the next John Doerr, the other two winning groups are accessible. Understanding how to access them is the objective we will tackle next.

### How to Become a Winner in the New Economy

I have identified two groups poised to thrive that I believe are accessible: those who can work creatively with intelligent machines and those who are stars in their fields. What’s the key to entering these lucrative sectors of the widening digital divide? I argue that two core abilities are crucial.

### Two Core Abilities for Thriving in the New Economy

1. The ability to quickly master hard things.

2. The ability to produce at an elite level, in terms of both quality and speed.

Let’s begin with the first ability. We must recognize that we have been spoiled by the intuitive and user-friendly experiences offered by many consumer-facing technologies. However, most of the intelligent machines driving the Great Restructuring are significantly more complex to understand and master.

Generating data-driven election forecasts is not as simple as typing “Who will win more votes?” into a search box. Instead, he maintains a vast database of poll results—thousands of polls from over 250 pollsters—that he inputs into Stata, a popular statistical analysis software. These are not easy tools to master.

Continuing with our Nate Silver case study, consider the other technology he relies on: Stata. This powerful tool is certainly not something one can learn intuitively after a bit of casual experimentation. Mastering how to manipulate these databases is a nuanced skill.

This approach of batching tasks enhances productivity and allows for high-quality work. By isolating research periods, one minimizes interruptions and maximizes cognitive resources, enabling deep dives into complex topics without distractions.

In conclusion, mastering the art of deep work is essential for anyone aspiring to thrive in today’s fast-paced, technology-driven world. It enables individuals to learn complex skills quickly and produce high-quality work consistently. The journey to mastery is not just about acquiring knowledge but also about cultivating the discipline to focus and produce at an elite level.

### High-Quality Work Produced = (Time Spent) x (Intensity of Focus)

If we accept this formula, Grant’s habits become clear: by maximizing his focus during work sessions, he enhances the results he achieves for each hour spent working.

This formulaic view of productivity isn’t new to me. I first encountered it while researching my second book, *How to Become a Straight-A Student*. During that research, I interviewed around fifty high-achieving college students from some of the nation’s most competitive universities. One observation that stood out was that the top students often studied less than those just below them in GPA rankings. The explanation for this phenomenon aligned with the formula I mentioned earlier: the best students recognized the importance of intensity in productivity and actively sought to maximize their concentration. As a result, they significantly reduced the time needed to prepare for exams or write papers, all without compromising the quality of their work.

Adam Grant’s example suggests that this intensity principle extends beyond undergraduate GPAs and applies to other cognitively demanding tasks. But why is this the case? An intriguing explanation comes from Sophie Leroy, a business professor at the University of Minnesota. In her 2009 paper, “Why Is It So Hard to Do My Work?”, Leroy introduced the concept of attention residue. She noted that while researchers have examined the effects of multitasking—attempting to juggle multiple tasks simultaneously—many professionals in knowledge work often switch between projects sequentially. “Going from one meeting to the next, starting to work on one project and soon after having to transition to another is just part of life in organizations,” Leroy explains.

The issue identified in her research is that when you switch from Task A to Task B, your attention doesn’t immediately follow; a residue of your focus remains stuck on the original task. This residue becomes particularly pronounced if your work on Task A was unstructured and lacked intensity before the switch. Even if you complete Task A before moving on, your attention remains divided for a while.

Leroy studied the impact of attention residue on performance by orchestrating task switches in a laboratory setting. In one experiment, she had participants work on a set of word puzzles. At some point, she interrupted them and instructed them to transition to a new and challenging task—reading résumés and making hypothetical hiring decisions. In other trials, she allowed participants to finish the puzzles before introducing the next task. Between the two tasks, she employed a quick lexical decision game to measure the amount of attention residue left from the first task. The results were clear: “People experiencing attention residue after switching tasks are likely to demonstrate poor performance on that next task,” and the more intense the residue, the worse the performance.

The concept of attention residue helps clarify why the intensity formula holds true and, in turn, sheds light on Grant’s productivity. By dedicating extended periods to a single challenging task without switching, Grant minimizes the detrimental effects of attention residue from other responsibilities, allowing him to maximize his performance on that one task. When Grant isolates himself for days to work on a paper, he operates at a higher level of effectiveness compared to a typical professor who follows a more distracted approach, frequently interrupted by attention-sapping distractions.

Even if you can’t fully replicate Grant’s extreme isolation—something I’ll address with alternative strategies for scheduling deep work in Part 2—the concept of attention residue is still significant. It suggests that the common habit of working in a state of semi-distraction can severely undermine your performance. It may seem harmless to check your inbox every ten minutes, but Leroy’s research indicates that this is not a substantial improvement. Each quick check introduces a new target for your attention. Worse yet, seeing messages that you can’t address immediately forces you to return to your primary task with a secondary task left unresolved. The attention residue from such unresolved switches diminishes your performance.

To perform at your peak level, you need to work for extended periods with full concentration on a single task, free from distractions. In other words, the type of work that optimizes your performance is deep work. If you’re uncomfortable engaging in deep work for long stretches, it will be challenging to elevate your performance to the high levels of quality and quantity that are increasingly necessary to succeed professionally. Unless your talents and skills far exceed those of your competition, the deep workers among them will outproduce you.

Now, I’ve laid out my argument for why deep work supports abilities that are becoming increasingly vital in our economy. However, before we fully accept this conclusion, we must address a common question: What about Jack Dorsey?

Jack Dorsey, co-founder of Twitter and Square, is often described as a “disrupter on a massive scale.” He doesn’t spend much time engaged in deep work. Dorsey’s responsibilities at both companies mean he doesn’t have the luxury of long, uninterrupted thinking sessions. His tightly calibrated schedule ensures that both companies maintain a predictable “weekly cadence,” leading to a fragmented allocation of his time and attention.

Dorsey has mentioned that he ends an average day with thirty to forty sets of meeting notes that he reviews and filters at night. He believes in serendipitous availability, stating, “I do a lot of my work at stand-up tables, which anyone can come up to. I get to hear all these conversations around the company.” This style of work lacks depth. Dorsey’s attention residue is likely thick as he moves from one meeting to another, allowing interruptions during the brief moments in between. Yet, we cannot label Dorsey’s work as shallow, because shallow work is low value and easily replicable. What Dorsey does is incredibly valuable and highly rewarded in our economy; as of this writing, he ranks among the top one thousand richest people in the world, with a net worth exceeding $1.1 billion.

Dorsey serves as a crucial example because he represents a group we cannot overlook: individuals who thrive without engaging in deep work. When I posed the question “What About Jack Dorsey?”, I was addressing a broader inquiry: If deep work is so essential, why do some distracted individuals succeed? 

First, we must acknowledge that Jack Dorsey is a high-level executive at two large companies. Individuals in such positions often thrive in environments that are inherently distracted. Kerry Trainor, CEO of Vimeo, provides insight into this phenomenon when he discusses how long he can go without checking email: “I can go a good solid Saturday without… well, most of the daytime without it… I mean, I’ll check it, but I won’t necessarily respond.”

At the same time, it’s important to recognize that these executives are better compensated and hold more significance in the American economy today than ever before. Dorsey’s success without deep work is common among elite management. However, acknowledging this reality doesn’t diminish the overall value of depth. The necessity for distraction in these executives’ work lives is highly specific to their roles. A good chief executive acts as a hard-to-automate decision engine. They possess a wealth of experience and have honed their instincts for their market. Throughout the day, they receive various inputs—emails, meetings, site visits—that they must process and act upon. Asking a CEO to spend four hours deeply contemplating a single problem would waste their unique value. It’s more efficient to hire three intelligent subordinates to think deeply about the issue and present their solutions to the executive for a final decision.

This specificity indicates that if you’re a high-level executive at a major company, the advice in the following pages may not apply to you. Conversely, it also suggests that you cannot generalize the approaches of these executives to other professions. Just because Dorsey embraces interruptions doesn’t mean you’ll achieve similar success by mimicking his behaviors; their actions are tailored to their specific roles as corporate leaders.

This principle of specificity should be applied to other counterexamples that may arise while reading this book. There are indeed sectors of our economy where depth is undervalued. In addition to executives, certain types of salespeople and lobbyists thrive on constant connectivity, which they consider their most valuable currency. Some individuals even manage to achieve success in fields where depth would be beneficial, despite their distracted approaches.

However, don’t be too quick to label your job as inherently non-deep. Just because your current habits make deep work challenging doesn’t mean that this lack of depth is essential for performing well in your role. In the next chapter, I’ll share the story of a group of high-powered management consultants who believed that constant email connectivity was crucial for servicing their clients. When a Harvard professor encouraged them to disconnect more regularly as part of a research study, they were surprised to discover that this connectivity wasn’t nearly as vital as they had assumed. Their clients didn’t need to reach them at all times, and their performance as consultants improved once their attention became less fragmented.

In summary, while deep work is not the only valuable skill in our economy, and it’s possible to succeed without cultivating this ability, the opportunities for doing so are increasingly rare. Unless you have compelling evidence that distraction is essential for your specific profession, you’re best served by giving serious consideration to the importance of depth.

### The Culture of Connectivity

A culture of connectivity simplifies life by fostering an environment where managing your day based on your inbox becomes acceptable. Responding promptly to the latest message while allowing others to accumulate behind it creates a false sense of productivity. If email were to take a backseat, one would need to adopt a more thoughtful approach to prioritize tasks and allocate time effectively. This kind of planning is challenging. For instance, David Allen’s *Getting Things Done* methodology proposes a complex flowchart to help decide what to tackle next. It’s far easier to chime in on the latest email thread.

While constant connectivity serves as a case study, it is just one of many business behaviors that undermine depth and likely diminish a company’s bottom line. These behaviors thrive because, in the absence of metrics, most people default to what is easiest.

Another example is the common practice of scheduling regular project meetings. These meetings accumulate and disrupt schedules, making sustained focus nearly impossible. They persist because they are easier; for many, these recurring meetings provide a blunt method of personal organization. Instead of managing their time independently, individuals allow the impending weekly meeting to compel them into action, creating a visible semblance of progress.

Consider the frustrating practice of forwarding emails with vague prompts like “Thoughts?” These messages take seconds to compose but can demand significant time and attention from recipients. A little more thoughtfulness could significantly reduce the overall time spent by everyone involved. Yet, from the sender’s perspective, these emails are simply easier—a way to clear something from their inbox with minimal effort.

The Principle of Least Resistance supports work cultures that spare us from the discomfort of concentration and planning, ultimately at the expense of long-term satisfaction and real value generation. This principle drives us toward shallow work in an economy that increasingly rewards depth. However, it is not the only trend exploiting the metric black hole to diminish depth. We must also consider the ever-present demand for “productivity.”

### Busyness as a Proxy for Productivity

Being a professor at a research-oriented university offers clarity in measuring success: Are you publishing important papers? This can even be quantified into a single number, such as the h-index, which reflects one’s impact on the field. For instance, in computer science, an h-index above 40 is notable, while single-digit scores during tenure reviews indicate trouble. Google Scholar automatically calculates your h-index, reminding you of your standing.

This clarity simplifies decision-making regarding work habits. The late physicist Richard Feynman explained his unconventional productivity strategy: “To do real good physics work, you need absolute solid lengths of time… it needs a lot of concentration.” He actively avoided administrative duties, understanding they would detract from his focus on producing high-quality work. Clarity about what is important provides clarity about what is not.

Professors are somewhat exceptional among knowledge workers, most of whom lack transparency regarding their performance. Social critic Matthew Crawford describes this uncertainty well: “Managers themselves inhabit a bewildering psychic landscape.” This applies to many roles in the sector. In his book *Shop Class as Soulcraft*, Crawford recounts leaving a director position to open a motorcycle repair shop, seeking tangible accomplishment that he struggled to replicate in a job centered around reports.

Many knowledge workers want to demonstrate productivity but lack clarity on what that entails. They don’t have an h-index or a collection of repaired motorcycles to showcase their worth. To bridge this gap, many revert to the last era when productivity was observable: the industrial age.

The rise of assembly lines coincided with the Efficiency Movement, led by Frederic Taylor, who monitored worker movements to enhance task completion speed. In Taylor’s time, productivity was clear-cut: widgets produced per unit of time. Today, many knowledge workers, lacking alternative metrics, gravitate back toward this outdated definition of productivity. David Allen even uses the phrase “cranking widgets” to describe a productive workflow. Knowledge workers increasingly lean toward visible busyness because they lack better means of demonstrating their value.

### The Cult of the Internet

Consider Alissa Rubin, the New York Times bureau chief in Paris. Before this role, she reported from Kabul, covering postwar reconstruction. At the time of writing, she was publishing articles examining the French government's complicity in the Rwandan genocide. Despite her serious journalism, she engages with Twitter, likely at her employer's urging.

Rubin’s Twitter profile shows a sporadic stream of posts, typically referencing articles she appreciates. Her true value lies in cultivating sources, gathering facts, and writing impactful articles. This raises an important question: Why is she encouraged to interrupt her essential deep work to generate superficial content for a platform managed by an unrelated media company?

The answer lies in a troubling relationship with technology. Neil Postman warned in the 1990s that society was developing a technopoly, assuming high-tech equated to good. He cautioned that this culture eliminates alternatives, making them invisible and irrelevant. Today, we equate “the Internet” with the future of business and government, idolizing digital innovations as symbols of progress while ignoring their potential drawbacks.

### Internet-Centrism and Deep Work

This Internet-centrism exemplifies what technopoly looks like today. Recognizing this reality clarifies the question that opened this section. The New York Times maintains a social media desk and pressures its writers, like Alissa Rubin, to engage in distracting behaviors because, within an Internet-centric technopoly, such actions are not subject to debate. The alternative—failing to embrace all things Internet—is, as Postman would argue, “invisible and therefore irrelevant.”

This invisibility helps explain the uproar when Jonathan Franzen suggested that novelists should refrain from tweeting. The backlash arose not from a deep understanding of book marketing or disagreement with Franzen’s conclusion, but from the shock that anyone serious would question the relevance of social media. In an Internet-centric technopoly, such a statement is akin to flag burning—an act of desecration rather than a topic for discussion.

The near-universal reach of this mindset was illustrated during my commute to the Georgetown campus. While waiting for the light to change at Connecticut Avenue, I idled behind a truck from a refrigerated supply chain logistics company. This industry, a quintessential old-school sector, starkly contrasts with the lean, consumer-facing tech startups currently in the spotlight. However, what struck me was a graphic affixed to the back of the truck: “Like us on Facebook.”

Deep work faces significant disadvantages in a technopoly because it is built on values such as quality, craftsmanship, and mastery—qualities that are decidedly old-fashioned. Supporting deep work often necessitates rejecting much of what is new and high-tech. Deep work is sidelined in favor of distracting high-tech behaviors, not because the former is inferior, but due to the absence of concrete metrics relating these behaviors to the bottom line. This creates a black hole of clarity, allowing us to elevate all things Internet into Morozov’s feared “uber-ideology.” In this culture, it is unsurprising that deep work struggles against the allure of tweets, likes, and posts.

Deep work should be a priority in today’s business climate, yet it is not. Various explanations exist for this paradox: deep work is challenging while shallow work is easier, the visible busyness surrounding shallow work becomes self-preserving, and our culture fosters the belief that any behavior associated with “the Internet” is inherently good—regardless of its impact on our ability to produce valuable work. These trends are exacerbated by the difficulty of measuring the value of depth or the costs of neglecting it.

For individuals who value depth, this reality spells bad news for businesses, leading them to overlook significant increases in value production. However, the shortsightedness of peers and employers reveals a personal advantage. As depth becomes increasingly rare, it will also become more valuable. With this understanding, you can confidently pursue the ultimate goal of this book: to systematically develop your ability to engage in deep work and reap substantial rewards.

### The Experience Sampling Method and Flow

When subjects recorded their feelings in real-time, Csikszentmihalyi and Larson gathered accurate data on how different activities influenced emotional states. This innovative approach led to the discovery of "flow," a state characterized by complete immersion in an activity, where individuals lose track of time and experience profound satisfaction. Csikszentmihalyi found that flow occurs when a task is both challenging and aligned with one's skills, fostering deep concentration.

Csikszentmihalyi identified several key conditions that facilitate flow: 

1. Clear goals provide direction and purpose.
2. Immediate feedback allows for real-time performance adjustments.
3. A sense of personal control enhances the likelihood of entering a flow state.

When these conditions are met, individuals not only find joy in their work but also report higher levels of creativity and productivity.

The implications of Csikszentmihalyi's findings are profound, especially in knowledge work. In an era dominated by distractions, cultivating flow through deep work can significantly enhance well-being. By prioritizing tasks that challenge us and align with our skills, we create opportunities for flow, leading to a more fulfilling work experience.

Moreover, the psychological benefits of deep work extend beyond individual satisfaction. Engaging in flow-inducing activities fosters a sense of connection to our work and enhances relationships with colleagues. When fully present and engaged, we collaborate more effectively and contribute to a positive work environment.

Empirical measurements showed that individuals were happier at work than during relaxation. The ESM studies confirmed that the more flow experiences a person has in a week, the greater their overall life satisfaction. Humans thrive when deeply engaged in challenging activities.

The connection between deep work and flow is evident: deep work naturally fosters a flow state. Csikszentmihalyi suggests that modern companies should adapt to this reality, advocating for job redesigns that resemble flow activities. Ultimately, structuring your working life around the experience of flow generated by deep work is a proven pathway to profound satisfaction. 

### A Philosophical Argument for Depth

Our final argument connecting depth to meaning requires a philosophical perspective. I turn to two scholars: Hubert Dreyfus and Sean Dorrance Kelly. In 2011, they published *All Things Shining*, examining how concepts of sacredness and meaning have evolved throughout human history, concerned about its trajectory in our current era.

### The Shift from Sacredness to Nihilism

“The world used to be, in its various forms, a world of sacred, shining things,” Dreyfus and Kelly state early in their book. “The shining things now seem far away.”

What led to this shift? The authors argue that the short answer lies with Descartes. From Descartes’s skepticism emerged the radical belief that the individual seeking certainty superseded a God or king providing truth. This Enlightenment thinking, while it led to the concept of human rights and liberated many from oppression, also stripped the world of the order and sacredness essential for creating meaning. In a post-Enlightenment world, we have taken on the task of determining what is meaningful and what is not, a process that can feel arbitrary and lead to a creeping nihilism. “The Enlightenment’s metaphysical embrace of the autonomous individual leads not just to a boring life,” Dreyfus and Kelly caution; “it leads almost inevitably to a nearly unlivable one.”

At first glance, this problem might seem distant from our exploration of professional satisfaction, but as we delve into Dreyfus and Kelly’s proposed solution, we will uncover valuable insights into the sources of meaning in our work. Their response to modern nihilism builds on the very subject that opened this chapter: craftsmanship.

Dreyfus and Kelly argue that craftsmanship offers a key to restoring a sense of sacredness in a responsible manner. They illustrate this claim with the example of a master wheelwright—the now-extinct profession of crafting wooden wagon wheels. “Because each piece of wood is distinct, it has its own personality,” they write. “The woodworker has an intimate relationship with the wood he works. Its subtle virtues call out to be cultivated and cared for.” In this appreciation for the “subtle virtues” of his medium, the craftsman discovers a source of meaning that exists outside the individual. The wheelwright does not arbitrarily decide which virtues of the wood are valuable; that value is inherent in the wood and the task it is meant to fulfill.

As Dreyfus and Kelly explain, such sacredness is a hallmark of craftsmanship. They conclude that the craftsman’s role is not to generate meaning but to cultivate the skill of discerning the meanings that are already present. This perspective liberates the craftsman from the nihilism of autonomous individualism, providing an ordered world rich in meaning. Moreover, this meaning is arguably safer than the sources cited in previous eras. The wheelwright, the authors imply, cannot easily use the inherent qualities of a piece of pine to justify a despotic monarchy.

Returning to the question of professional satisfaction, Dreyfus and Kelly’s interpretation of craftsmanship as a pathway to meaning offers a nuanced understanding of why the work of individuals like Ric Furrer resonates with so many of us. The satisfaction evident on Furrer’s face as he extracts artistry from raw metals reflects an appreciation for something elusive yet valuable in modernity: a glimpse of the sacred.

Once we grasp this sacredness inherent in traditional craftsmanship, we can connect it to the realm of knowledge work. To do this, we must first make two key observations. The first is straightforward yet essential: there is nothing intrinsic to manual trades that uniquely generates this particular source of meaning. Any pursuit—whether physical or cognitive—that supports high levels of skill can also cultivate a sense of sacredness.

To illustrate this point, let’s transition from traditional examples of woodworking or metalworking to the modern field of computer programming. Consider the words of coding prodigy Santiago Gonzalez, who described his work: “Beautiful code is short and concise, so if you were to give that code to another programmer they would say, ‘oh, that’s well-written code.’ It’s much like if you were writing a poem.” Gonzalez speaks about programming in a manner reminiscent of how woodworkers discuss their craft.

The Pragmatic Programmer draws a direct connection between coding and traditional craftsmanship by quoting the medieval quarry worker’s creed: “We who cut mere stones must always be envisioning cathedrals.” The book elaborates that computer programmers must adopt a similar mindset: “Within the overall structure of a project, there is always room for individuality and craftsmanship… One hundred years from now, our engineering may seem as archaic as the techniques used by medieval cathedral builders seem to today’s civil engineers, while our craftsmanship will still be honored.”

In essence, you don’t need to be laboring in an open-air barn for your efforts to be recognized as craftsmanship that can yield meaning. A similar potential for craftsmanship exists in most skilled jobs within the information economy. Whether you are a writer, marketer, consultant, or lawyer, your work is a craft. If you refine your abilities and apply them with respect and care, you can, like the skilled wheelwright, uncover meaning in the daily efforts of your professional life.

Some may argue that their knowledge work cannot possibly become a source of meaning because the subject matter is too mundane. However, this line of thinking is flawed. In contemporary culture, we place significant emphasis on job descriptions. Our obsession with the advice to “follow your passion” stems from the misguided belief that the specifics of the job you choose are paramount for career satisfaction. This perspective suggests that only certain prestigious jobs can provide fulfillment, while all others are deemed soulless and bland.

The philosophy of Dreyfus and Kelly liberates us from such constraints. The craftsmen they reference did not hold glamorous jobs. For most of human history, being a blacksmith or wheelwright was not considered prestigious. Yet this distinction is irrelevant; the meaning derived from such efforts arises from the skill and appreciation inherent in craftsmanship—not the outcomes of their work. In other words, while a wooden wheel may not be noble, the act of shaping it can be. The same principle applies to knowledge work. You don’t need a prestigious job; instead, you need a refined approach to your work.

The second key observation is that cultivating craftsmanship is inherently a deep task, necessitating a commitment to deep work. Deep work is crucial for extracting meaning from your profession in the manner described by Dreyfus and Kelly. Embracing deep work in your career and directing it toward skill cultivation can transform a knowledge work job from a distracted, draining obligation into something fulfilling—a gateway to a world filled with shining, wondrous things.

### Introduction

Before diving into these strategies, I want to address a lingering question: Why do we need elaborate interventions for deep work? Once you recognize its value, isn’t it enough to simply do more of it? Do we really require something as complex as the Eudaimonia Machine to remember to concentrate?

Unfortunately, replacing distraction with focus is not that simple. One primary obstacle to achieving depth is the compulsion to divert attention toward something superficial. While most people acknowledge that this urge complicates their ability to concentrate on challenging tasks, many underestimate its frequency and intensity.

Consider a 2012 study led by psychologists Wilhelm Hofmann and Roy Baumeister, which outfitted 205 adults with beepers that activated at random intervals. When the beeper sounded, participants paused to reflect on desires they were experiencing or had felt in the last thirty minutes. After a week, the researchers collected over 7,500 samples. The findings were clear: people grapple with desires throughout the day. As Baumeister summarized in his book, *Willpower*: “Desire turned out to be the norm, not the exception.”

The five most common desires participants struggled against included eating, sleeping, and sex. However, the list also featured desires for “taking a break from work, checking email and social networking sites, surfing the web, listening to music, or watching television.” The allure of the Internet and television was particularly potent; subjects resisted these distractions only about half the time.

These results pose a significant challenge to cultivating a deep work habit. They indicate that throughout the day, you can expect to be bombarded with the desire to engage in anything but deep work. You might argue that you will succeed where these subjects faltered because you understand the importance of depth. While this sentiment is commendable, decades of research highlight its futility. A substantial body of work, initiated by pioneering papers from Baumeister, has established a crucial truth about willpower: it is finite and becomes depleted with use.

Your willpower functions more like a muscle that tires rather than an endless reservoir. This explains why the subjects in the Hofmann and Baumeister study struggled to resist their desires—over time, distractions drained their limited willpower. The same will occur for you unless you are strategic about your habits.

### Developing a Deep Work Habit

The key to developing a deep work habit is to move beyond mere good intentions and incorporate routines and rituals that minimize the willpower required to transition into and maintain uninterrupted concentration. For instance, if you decide in the middle of a distracted afternoon to switch your focus to a cognitively demanding task, you will heavily draw from your finite willpower. Such attempts will frequently fail. Conversely, if you establish smart routines—like designating a specific time and quiet location for deep tasks—you will require significantly less willpower to initiate and sustain your focus. Over time, this approach will lead to greater success in your deep work efforts.

With this understanding, the following strategies can be viewed as routines designed with the science of limited willpower in mind, aimed at maximizing your deep work accomplishments. They will encourage you to commit to a specific pattern for scheduling this work and develop rituals to enhance your concentration before each session.

### Decide on Your Depth Philosophy

You need to establish your own philosophy for integrating deep work into your professional life. Attempting to schedule deep work ad hoc is not effective. This example serves as a warning: choose a philosophy that aligns with your specific circumstances, as a mismatch can derail your deep work habit before it solidifies. 

I have observed four distinct depth philosophies that work exceptionally well in practice. The goal is to demonstrate that there are numerous ways to incorporate deep work into your schedule, making it worthwhile to find an approach that resonates with you.

### The Monastic Philosophy of Deep Work Scheduling

Donald Knuth, a renowned computer scientist, embodies the monastic philosophy of deep work scheduling. He seeks to maximize deep efforts by eliminating or drastically reducing shallow obligations. Knuth articulates his professional goal as striving to learn certain areas of computer science exhaustively and digest that knowledge for others.

Another individual who embraces this philosophy is science fiction writer Neal Stephenson. He succinctly summarizes his communication policy: “Persons who wish to interfere with my concentration are politely requested not to do so.” Stephenson prioritizes high-quality novel production over responding to emails or attending conferences, which would fragment his time and diminish his productivity.

The monastic philosophy often makes knowledge workers defensive. The clarity with which its practitioners define their value can strike a nerve among those whose contributions are more complex. If you belong to this group—someone whose contributions are discrete and individualized—you should consider adopting this philosophy, as it could elevate your career from average to memorable.

### The Bimodal Philosophy of Deep Work Scheduling

Carl Jung exemplifies the bimodal philosophy of deep work. In the 1920s, he began regular retreats to a rustic stone house, locking himself in a minimally furnished room each morning to write without interruption. Unlike Knuth and Stephenson, Jung did not seek to eliminate distractions entirely; he only pursued this during his retreats. The rest of his time was spent in Zurich, where he ran a busy clinical practice and participated in the vibrant coffeehouse culture.

The bimodal philosophy advocates for dividing your time into clearly defined stretches dedicated to deep pursuits while leaving the rest open to other activities. During deep work periods, the bimodal worker adopts a monastic mindset, seeking intense concentration. This division can occur on various scales, allowing for the fulfillment of both deep and shallow work needs.

### The Bimodal Philosophy of Deep Work

A modern example of the bimodal philosophy in action is Adam Grant, the Wharton Business School professor whose thoughtful approach to work habits I introduced in Part 1. Grant’s schedule during his rapid ascent at Wharton serves as an excellent case study in bimodality. He clustered his courses into one semester, allowing him to focus on deep work during the other. Within these deep semesters, he would occasionally immerse himself in monastic work for two to four days, shutting his door, activating an out-of-office auto-responder, and dedicating himself to uninterrupted research. Outside of these sessions, Grant remained famously accessible, a necessity given that his 2013 bestseller, *Give and Take*, advocates for giving time and attention without expecting anything in return as a key strategy for professional advancement.

Those who adopt the bimodal philosophy admire the productivity of monastics while recognizing the value of shallow behaviors in their professional lives. The primary challenge in implementing this philosophy is that even brief periods of deep work require a level of flexibility that many fear they lack. If an hour away from your inbox feels daunting, the idea of disappearing for a day or more may seem impossible. However, I suspect that bimodal working is compatible with more job types than one might initially think.

Earlier, I referenced a study by Harvard Business School professor Leslie Perlow, where management consultants were asked to disconnect for a full day each week. Initially, they feared negative client reactions, but clients turned out to be indifferent. As Jung, Grant, and Perlow’s subjects discovered, people generally respect your right to become inaccessible if these periods are well-defined and clearly communicated, while remaining readily available outside of them.

### The Rhythmic Philosophy of Deep Work Scheduling

In the early days of *Seinfeld*, Jerry Seinfeld was a working comic with a hectic tour schedule. A writer named Brad Isaac, performing at open mic nights, encountered Seinfeld and asked for tips. Seinfeld advised that to be a better comic, one must create better jokes, which requires writing every day. He shared a technique: keeping a calendar on his wall and crossing out each day he wrote jokes with a big red X. “After a few days, you’ll have a chain,” he said. “Just keep at it, and the chain will grow longer every day. Your only job next is to not break the chain.”

This chain method quickly gained popularity among writers and fitness enthusiasts, serving as a concrete example of a broader approach to integrating depth into life: the rhythmic philosophy. This philosophy posits that the easiest way to initiate deep work sessions is to transform them into a simple, regular habit. The goal is to establish a rhythm that eliminates the need to decide when and if to engage in deep work.

Another common way to implement the rhythmic philosophy is to set a specific starting time for deep work each day. Just as visual indicators can lower the barrier to entry for deep work, eliminating scheduling decisions can facilitate a smoother transition into focused efforts.

### Establishing a Deep Work Ritual

To create a structured environment that fosters deep work, consider the following elements:

- **Where you’ll work:** Choose a space that minimizes distractions and signals to your brain that it’s time to focus.

- **When you’ll work:** Establish a consistent schedule for your deep work sessions, allowing your mind to anticipate these periods.

- **How you’ll prepare:** Include preparatory steps that signal the beginning of deep work, such as making a cup of tea or turning off notifications.

- **What tools you’ll use:** Decide in advance what materials or tools you’ll need, minimizing interruptions.

- **How you’ll reward yourself:** Implement a reward system after completing a deep work session, reinforcing the habit.

By establishing a ritual that addresses these questions, you create an environment conducive to deep work. The more you adhere to your ritual, the more automatic the transition into deep work becomes, maximizing productivity and creativity.

### The Balance Between Deep Work and Collaboration

The relationship between deep work and collaboration is not inherently contradictory; they can complement each other when approached thoughtfully. While I may need uninterrupted time to dive deeply into a complex problem, discussing ideas with colleagues can provide fresh perspectives that enhance understanding.

To leverage collaboration effectively, I recommend establishing clear boundaries around deep work sessions. Schedule specific times for focused work and reserve other times for collaborative discussions. This protects concentration while still benefiting from insights that arise from working with others.

The quality of collaboration significantly impacts the effectiveness of deep work. Engaging with individuals who share a commitment to depth creates an environment where ideas flourish. Thus, being selective about collaborators and ensuring purposeful interactions can enhance both focused effort and creative collaboration.

### The Role of Collaboration in Deep Work

The division of efforts in innovation does not tell the whole story. Even when retreating to a spoke, working in isolation is not always the best approach. Take the invention of the point-contact transistor at Bell Labs. This breakthrough was made possible by a large group of researchers, each with distinct specialties, who collaborated within the solid-state physics research group—a team dedicated to creating a smaller, more reliable alternative to the vacuum tube. Their collaborative discussions were essential precursors to the transistor, exemplifying the value of hub behavior.

Once the research group established the intellectual foundation for the component, the innovation process shifted to a spoke. What makes this case particularly interesting is that even in this phase, collaboration remained key. It was two researchers—experimentalist Walter Brattain and quantum theorist John Bardeen—who, over the course of a month in 1947, made the series of breakthroughs that led to the first working solid-state transistor.

Brattain and Bardeen worked closely together in a small lab, often side by side, challenging each other to refine their designs. Their efforts primarily involved deep work, but of a type we have yet to discuss. Brattain would intensely focus on engineering an experimental design that leveraged Bardeen’s latest theoretical insights, while Bardeen would concentrate on interpreting the results of Brattain’s experiments, striving to expand his theoretical framework to align with the observations. This back-and-forth exemplifies a collaborative form of deep work that I refer to as the whiteboard effect. For certain problems, collaborating with someone at a shared whiteboard can propel you deeper into the subject than if you were working alone. The presence of another person, whether physically in the same room or collaborating virtually, can short-circuit the instinct to avoid depth.

From this, we can draw practical conclusions about the role of collaboration in deep work. The experiences at Building 20 and Bell Labs suggest that isolation is not a prerequisite for productive deep work. In fact, their examples indicate that for many types of work—especially when pursuing innovation—collaborative deep work can yield superior results. This approach encourages you to consider collaboration as you contemplate how best to integrate depth into your professional life. However, two guidelines should be kept in mind.

First, distraction is a significant barrier to depth. Therefore, the hub-and-spoke model serves as a vital framework. Separate your pursuit of serendipitous encounters from your efforts to think deeply and build upon those inspirations. Aim to optimize each endeavor independently, rather than merging them into a muddled mix that hinders both objectives.

Second, even when you retreat to a spoke for deep thinking, leverage the whiteboard effect when it makes sense to do so. Collaborating side by side with someone on a problem can drive you both toward deeper insights, ultimately leading to more valuable outcomes compared to working in isolation.

In summary, when it comes to deep work, consider collaboration when appropriate, as it can elevate your results. Simultaneously, avoid romanticizing the quest for interaction and positive randomness to the extent that it undermines the uninterrupted concentration necessary to extract meaningful insights from the ideas swirling around us.

### Execute Like a Business

In the mid-1990s, Harvard Business School professor Clayton Christensen received a call from Andy Grove, the CEO of Intel. Grove had encountered Christensen’s research on disruptive innovation and invited him to California to discuss its implications for Intel. Upon arrival, Christensen explained the fundamentals of disruption: established companies are often unexpectedly overthrown by startups that begin with low-cost offerings at the bottom of the market but gradually improve their products enough to capture high-end market share. Grove recognized that Intel faced this threat from low-end processors produced by emerging companies like AMD and Cyrix. Armed with this newfound understanding, Grove devised the strategy that led to the Celeron family of processors—a lower-performance option that helped Intel fend off challenges from below.

However, during a break in their meeting, Grove asked Christensen, “How do I do this?” Christensen began discussing business strategy, but Grove interrupted him: “You are such a naïve academic. I asked you how to do it, and you told me what I should do. I know what I need to do. I just don’t know how to do it.”

This distinction between what and how is crucial yet often overlooked in the professional world. Identifying a necessary strategy to achieve a goal is typically straightforward, but the challenge lies in executing that strategy once it has been identified. I came across this story in a foreword Christensen wrote for a book titled The 4 Disciplines of Execution, which outlines four “disciplines” designed to help companies successfully implement high-level strategies. As I read, I realized that the gap between what and how was relevant to my personal quest to dedicate more time to deep work. Just as Andy Grove recognized the importance of competing in the low-end processor market, I had identified the significance of prioritizing depth. What I needed was guidance on how to execute this strategy.

Intrigued by these parallels, I set out to adapt the 4DX framework to my personal work habits and was pleasantly surprised by how effective they proved in steering me toward meaningful action on my goal of working deeply. Although these ideas were originally crafted for the corporate world, the underlying concepts seem applicable to any situation where something important needs to be accomplished amidst numerous competing obligations and distractions. 

### Discipline #1: Focus on the Wildly Important

The authors of The 4 Disciplines of Execution assert, “The more you try to do, the less you actually accomplish.” They elaborate that execution should concentrate on a small number of “wildly important goals.” This simplicity helps focus an organization’s energy with sufficient intensity to ignite real results.

For an individual aiming to engage in deep work, this implies that you should identify a limited number of ambitious outcomes to pursue during your deep work hours. The general call to “spend more time working deeply” lacks the ability to inspire. Instead, having a specific goal that promises tangible and substantial professional benefits will generate a more consistent motivation. 

In my own experience, I found that taking breaks or stepping away from my work often led to breakthroughs that I wouldn’t have achieved through relentless focus. For instance, during my tenure as I worked on my ambitious goal of publishing five peer-reviewed papers, I noticed that moments of idleness—whether it was a walk outside or simply daydreaming—often sparked new ideas or solutions to problems I had been wrestling with. This aligns with research suggesting that downtime is not merely a luxury but a necessity for productive thought. 

### Reason #2: Downtime Enhances Productivity

Research indicates that taking regular breaks can lead to improved performance. For example, a study published in the journal Cognition found that participants who took breaks during a task performed better than those who worked continuously without interruption. This suggests that allowing your mind to rest can replenish your cognitive resources, making you more effective when you return to your work.

In my journey with 4DX, I implemented structured breaks into my schedule, which not only helped me maintain my focus during deep work sessions but also increased my overall output. By allowing myself time to recharge, I found that I could dive deeper into my work when I returned, ultimately leading to higher quality results.

### Reason #3: Downtime Fosters Creativity

Finally, downtime is essential for fostering creativity. When we allow our minds to wander, we create the conditions for new ideas to emerge. A study published in the journal Psychological Science found that participants who engaged in mind-wandering performed better on creative tasks than those who were focused on a specific goal. This underscores the importance of giving ourselves permission to step back from our work and let our minds roam freely.

In my own practice, I began to incorporate periods of unstructured time into my routine, where I would engage in activities unrelated to my academic work. Whether it was reading fiction, doodling, or simply staring out the window, these moments of creative idleness often led to unexpected insights that enriched my research.

In conclusion, embracing idleness and allowing for downtime is not just about avoiding burnout; it’s a strategic approach to enhancing productivity, fostering creativity, and ultimately achieving deeper work. By implementing structured breaks and periods of mental rest, I found that I could cultivate a more effective and fulfilling work habit. This shift not only improved my output but also transformed my relationship with work, allowing me to engage more deeply and meaningfully with my goals.

### The Importance of Evening Downtime

If you continuously interrupt your evening to check emails or dedicate hours after dinner to meet deadlines, you deprive your attention centers of the uninterrupted rest they need for restoration. Even brief incursions of work can hinder your ability to achieve deeper relaxation, essential for recharging your brain. Only by feeling confident that your work is complete for the day can you begin to downshift and prepare for the next. Attempting to squeeze in more work during your evenings may diminish your effectiveness the following day, resulting in less productivity than if you had respected a proper shutdown.

### The Value of Downtime

The final argument for maintaining a clear endpoint to your workday returns to Anders Ericsson, the pioneer of deliberate practice theory. Deliberate practice involves systematically stretching your abilities in a given skill and is essential for improvement. Deep work and deliberate practice overlap significantly, and for our purposes, we can use deliberate practice as a stand-in for cognitively demanding efforts.

In Ericsson’s seminal 1993 paper, “The Role of Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance,” he reviews research on individuals' capacity for cognitively demanding work. He notes that novices can typically concentrate intensely for about an hour a day, while experts can extend this to four hours—but rarely more.

One study he cites examines the practice habits of elite violinists at Berlin’s Universität der Künste. Elite players averaged around three and a half hours of deliberate practice each day, usually divided into two sessions. In contrast, less accomplished players spent less time in deep focus.

These findings imply that your capacity for deep work is limited each day. If you manage your schedule carefully—using productivity strategies—you should reach your daily deep work capacity during work hours. Consequently, by evening, you are likely past the point where you can effectively engage in deep work. Any tasks you attempt to fit into the night are unlikely to be high-value activities that significantly advance your career; instead, they will probably consist of low-value, shallow tasks executed at a slow, low-energy pace. By postponing evening work, you are not missing out on much of importance.

### Implementing a Shutdown Strategy

To implement a successful shutdown strategy, commit to ensuring that once your workday concludes, no professional concerns intrude upon your attention. This commitment includes refraining from checking emails or browsing work-related websites. Even a brief intrusion can trigger a cycle of distraction that undermines the shutdown advantages discussed earlier.

Another essential commitment is to establish a strict shutdown ritual at the end of your workday. This ritual should ensure that every incomplete task, goal, or project has been reviewed, confirming that either (1) you have a reliable plan for its completion or (2) it is documented for future revisiting. This process should follow a systematic algorithm: a series of steps you consistently execute in order. Once completed, conclude with a phrase that signifies the end of your workday (for me, I say, “Shutdown complete”). This final step serves as a cue to your mind that it is safe to release work-related thoughts for the remainder of the day.

For example, my shutdown ritual, developed while writing my doctoral dissertation, involves checking my email one last time, transferring new tasks into my official task lists, and reviewing my calendar. After ensuring I haven’t overlooked anything urgent, I create a rough plan for the next day and conclude with “Shutdown complete.”

### The Zeigarnik Effect and Task Management

The concept of a shutdown ritual may seem excessive, but it is grounded in the Zeigarnik effect, which describes how incomplete tasks can dominate our attention. If you simply stop working at 5 p.m. and declare, “I’m done until tomorrow,” unresolved obligations will compete for your attention throughout the evening.

Fortunately, we don’t need to complete every task to alleviate it from our minds. Psychologist Roy Baumeister’s research shows that formulating a plan for interrupted tasks can significantly mitigate the Zeigarnik effect. The shutdown ritual I described earlier leverages this strategy, ensuring that no task will be overlooked and each will be revisited daily.

While shutdown rituals may feel tedious, they are essential for reaping the benefits of systematic idleness. It typically takes a week or two for the shutdown habit to solidify, but once established, it will become a permanent fixture in your life.

### Embracing Boredom and Concentration Training

To succeed with deep work, you must rewire your brain to resist distracting stimuli. This doesn’t mean eliminating distractions entirely; it’s enough to remove their power to hijack your attention. Scheduling Internet use can significantly help regain this attention autonomy.

If you find yourself constantly attached to your smartphone or laptop, your behavior outside of work may undermine your efforts to rewire your brain. When planning your Internet use after work, allow for time-sensitive communication but otherwise, put your phone away and refrain from using the Internet.

In summary, embrace boredom and resist the urge to distract yourself. This shift won’t happen overnight, but with consistent effort, you’ll find yourself better equipped to engage in deep work, ultimately enhancing your productivity and creativity.

### Experiment with Roosevelt Dashes

Try this experiment no more than once a week at first, allowing your brain to practice intensity while also giving it time to recover. Once you feel confident in exchanging concentration for completion time, increase the frequency of these Roosevelt dashes. Always keep your self-imposed deadlines at the edge of feasibility. You should consistently beat the buzzer (or at least come close), but doing so should require significant concentration.

The motivation behind this strategy is clear. Deep work demands levels of concentration that exceed what most knowledge workers are accustomed to. Roosevelt dashes utilize artificial deadlines to systematically elevate your focus—essentially providing interval training for your brain's attention centers. An additional benefit is that these dashes are incompatible with distraction; you cannot give in to distractions and still meet your deadlines. Consequently, every completed dash represents a session where you might feel bored and crave novel stimuli, yet you resist. The more you practice resisting such urges, the easier it becomes.

After a few months of employing this strategy, your understanding of focus will likely transform as you reach levels of intensity stronger than anything you’ve previously experienced. If you’re anything like a young Roosevelt, you can then repurpose the extra free time toward the finer pleasures in life.

### Meditate Productively

During my two years as a postdoctoral associate at MIT, I developed a practice I now suggest you adopt: productive meditation. This involves utilizing periods when you’re physically occupied but not mentally engaged—like walking or jogging—to focus on a single, well-defined professional problem.

I practiced productive meditation during my daily cross-river treks in Boston, and as I honed this skill, my results improved. I encourage you to incorporate this practice into your life, aiming for at least two or three sessions each week. It capitalizes on otherwise wasted time and can enhance your professional productivity.

To succeed, recognize that productive meditation requires practice. Initially, I found myself easily distracted, often ending sessions with little to show for my efforts. It took about a dozen sessions before I began to see real results, so patience is crucial.

Be wary of distractions and looping. When your mind wanders, gently redirect your focus back to the problem. Looping, where your mind rehashes what you already know instead of diving deeper, can undermine your session. Stay vigilant against both distractions and looping to develop a productive meditation habit.

### The Challenge of Distraction

In my own journey, I have found that limiting my engagement with social media creates space for deeper thinking and more meaningful interactions. My productivity increases significantly when I resist the urge to check my feeds. The time I once spent scrolling can now be redirected toward activities that truly matter to me, whether that’s writing, reading, or reflecting on my day.

The addictive nature of these platforms poses a challenge. They are designed to keep us engaged, often at the expense of our focus and mental clarity. Setting boundaries is crucial. I designate specific times for checking emails and social media, rather than allowing them to intrude upon my work or personal time. This deliberate approach helps me maintain control over my attention and ensures I can dedicate myself fully to the tasks at hand.

The decision to quit or limit social media use is personal, but I encourage anyone struggling with concentration to consider the benefits of disconnecting. Like Baratunde Thurston, I’ve found that quiet moments away from digital noise can lead to greater creativity and productivity. By prioritizing deep work and minimizing distractions, I cultivate a more focused and fulfilling life.

To enhance your ability to work deeply, it’s essential to recognize how distractions undermine your efforts. If you continue to live like the pre-experiment Thurston, allowing your life to devolve into a chaotic mix of apps and browser tabs, implementing strategies to sharpen your focus will become significantly more challenging. Willpower is finite; the more enticing distractions you have vying for your attention, the harder it will be to concentrate on what truly matters. To master deep work, you must reclaim control over your time and attention.

Before combating distractions, we need to understand the landscape we’re navigating. Thurston’s experience illustrates the ineffectiveness with which knowledge workers currently address the challenges posed by network tools. Overwhelmed by these demands, he felt his only recourse was to temporarily quit the Internet altogether. This notion that a drastic Internet sabbatical is the sole alternative to distractions oversimplifies the problem.

The idea of quitting the Internet entirely is impractical for most people. No one is expected to follow Thurston’s lead, and this often leads to the acceptance of our distracted state as unavoidable. Despite the insights gained during his hiatus, it didn’t take long for him to revert to distraction.

This rule aims to break us free from this cycle by proposing a third option: recognizing that these tools are not inherently harmful but should be used with stricter criteria. Most people should use far fewer of these tools. I urge you to reject the state of distracted hyperconnectedness that drove Thurston to extreme measures. There is a middle ground, and if you’re serious about developing a deep work habit, you must strive to reach it.

### The Craftsman Approach to Tool Selection

To illustrate careful tool curation, it’s helpful to turn to someone who relies on a complex relationship with non-digital tools to succeed. Forrest Pritchard, a lanky English major turned sustainable farmer, exemplifies this.

Pritchard runs Smith Meadows, a family farm located an hour west of Washington, D.C., nestled in the Blue Ridge Mountains. After taking over the land from his parents, he transitioned from traditional monoculture crops to grass-finished meat. The farm bypasses wholesaling; you won’t find Smith Meadows steaks in Whole Foods. Instead, they sell directly to consumers at bustling farmers’ markets in the D.C. metro area. By all accounts, the farm thrives in an industry that rarely rewards small operations.

I first encountered Pritchard at our local farmers’ market in Takoma Park, Maryland, where the Smith Meadows stand does a brisk business. Seeing him—towering over most of his suburban customers, clad in the obligatory faded flannel of a farmer—was to witness a craftsman confident in his trade. I introduced myself because farming hinges on the careful management of tools, and I wanted to understand how a craftsman in a non-digital field approaches this critical task.

“Haymaking is a good example,” he told me early in our conversation. “It’s a subject where I can give you the basic idea without glossing over the underlying economics.”

When Pritchard took over Smith Meadows, the farm produced its own hay to feed animals during the winter months when grazing is impossible. Haymaking involves a hay baler, a device pulled behind a tractor that compresses and binds dried grass into bales. For East Coast farmers, owning a hay baler seems logical: your animals need hay. Why spend money on feed when you have grass growing for free? However, Pritchard explained that adopting such a simplistic mindset would lead to disaster. “I’d be counting the days until the ‘For Sale’ sign goes up on the property,” he said. 

Pritchard employs a more nuanced thought process when assessing tools. After careful consideration, he decided to sell the baler and now purchases all the hay used on the farm.

“Let’s start by exploring the costs of making hay,” Pritchard said. “First, there’s the actual cost of fuel, repairs, and the shed to store the baler. You also have to pay taxes on it.” These directly measurable costs were the easy part of his decision. The more challenging aspect was considering the opportunity costs. “If I make hay all summer, I can’t be doing something else. For example, I now use that time to raise broilers [chickens raised for meat]. These generate positive cash flow because I can sell them, and they also produce manure, which I can then use to enhance my soil.”

Pritchard also assessed the secondary value of a purchased bale of hay. “When I buy hay, I’m trading cash for animal protein, as well as manure, which means I’m also getting more nutrients for my land in exchange for my money. I’m also avoiding compacting soils by driving heavy machinery over my ground all summer long.”

Ultimately, Pritchard concluded that buying hay results in healthier fields. “Soil fertility is my baseline,” he summarized. By this calculation, the baler had to go.

Pritchard’s decision highlights an important reality: the idea that identifying some benefit is sufficient to justify investing time, money, and attention in a tool is laughable to people in his trade. Every tool at the farm supply store offers something useful, but it also comes with drawbacks. Pritchard began with a clear baseline—soil health—and built upon this foundation to arrive at a final decision regarding whether to use a particular tool.

If you’re a knowledge worker—especially one interested in cultivating a deep work habit—you should approach your tool selection with the same level of care as skilled workers like farmers. I propose a general assessment strategy, which I call the craftsman approach.

This approach encourages thoughtful evaluation of the tools you engage with, ensuring they align with your most important goals. By doing so, you reclaim control over your time and attention, allowing you to focus on what truly matters in both your professional endeavors and personal relationships. 

### Quit Social Media

When Ryan Nicodemus sought to simplify his life, one of his first targets was his possessions. Living alone in a spacious three-bedroom condo, he had filled the space driven by consumerist impulses. Now, he aimed to reclaim his life from his belongings. 

He spent an afternoon packing everything he owned into cardboard boxes as if preparing to move. To make this “difficult undertaking” more manageable, he dubbed it a “packing party,” reasoning, “Everything’s more exciting when it’s a party, right?”

Once the packing was complete, Nicodemus spent the following week going about his normal routine. If he needed something that was packed, he would unpack it and return it to its original place. At the end of the week, he realized that most of his belongings remained untouched in their boxes. So, he decided to get rid of them.

Stuff accumulates partly because it’s easy to worry, “What if I need this one day?” Nicodemus’s packing party provided clear evidence that most of his belongings were unnecessary, thus supporting his quest for simplicity.

### A Systematic Approach to Decluttering Social Media

The last strategy I propose offers a systematic method to help you sort through the network tools currently claiming your time and attention. Inspired by Ryan Nicodemus’s decluttering method, this approach involves a thirty-day social media detox.

Instead of “packing,” you’ll ban yourself from using services like Facebook, Instagram, Google+, Twitter, Snapchat, and Vine for thirty days. Don’t deactivate these accounts or announce your break online; simply stop using them cold turkey. If someone inquires about your decreased activity, you can explain, but don’t feel obligated to inform everyone.

After thirty days, reflect on these two questions for each service you temporarily quit:

1. Would the last thirty days have been significantly better if I had been able to use this service?
2. Did people care that I wasn’t using this service?

If you answer “no” to both, consider quitting the service permanently. If it’s a clear “yes,” feel free to return. For ambiguous answers, I encourage you to lean toward quitting, as you can always rejoin later.

This strategy specifically targets social media because these platforms can be particularly detrimental to your pursuit of deeper work if used without limits. They deliver personalized information on an unpredictable schedule, making them addictive and undermining your ability to concentrate. Knowledge workers, especially those whose livelihoods depend on deep work, should be wary of these tools. However, social media companies have executed a masterful marketing strategy, convincing our culture that not using their products means missing out.

This fear of missing out parallels Nicodemus’s anxiety about excess items in his closets, which is why I propose a corrective strategy akin to his packing party. A month without these services can replace your fear of missing out on events and conversations with the reality that they’re not as important as you might think.

I advise against announcing your thirty-day experiment because many people believe others want to hear their thoughts and might be disappointed by their absence. This sentiment is common and important to address. For example, the average Twitter user has 208 followers, leading many to believe their activities are significant. Speaking from experience, I can attest that this feeling is powerfully addictive.

Before social media, building an audience beyond friends and family required hard work. In the early 2000s, starting a blog meant delivering valuable information to attract even a handful of visitors. I launched my first blog in 2003, and it took years to grow an audience. Now, social media has short-circuited the connection between producing real value and gaining attention, replacing it with a shallow exchange: I’ll pay attention to you if you pay attention to me, regardless of value.

The experiments conducted by 37signals illustrate a crucial truth: the shallow work that occupies knowledge workers’ time is often less critical than it appears. By reducing shallow tasks and creating space for deep work, productivity and creativity can improve dramatically. It’s not just about working fewer hours; it’s about working smarter.

In my experience, limiting shallow work—like responding to emails or unnecessary meetings—allows me to dedicate more time to deep work, leading to greater satisfaction and achievement. This shift fosters a sense of accomplishment that shallow tasks cannot provide.

To maximize productivity and creativity, we must drain the shallows from our work lives. Prioritizing deep work while minimizing distractions unlocks our full potential.

However, there is a limit to this anti-shallow mindset. While deep work is valuable, some shallow work is necessary to sustain knowledge work roles. I may resist checking my email every ten minutes, but I can’t ignore important messages altogether. The goal is to manage shallow work rather than eliminate it entirely.

Cognitive capacity also plays a role. Deep work can be exhausting, and performance psychologists note that individuals can sustain such efforts for limited periods. For beginners, an hour a day is reasonable, while more experienced individuals can manage about four hours.

Once I reach my limit for deep work, pushing beyond that yields diminishing returns. Shallow work becomes detrimental when it encroaches upon my limited deep work hours. The typical workday spans eight hours, but if the most skilled deep thinker can only spend four hours in true depth, it’s easy for shallow tasks to consume the remaining time.

As a professor, my schedule is less burdened by commitments, yet they still consume significant portions of my time. Reflecting on a typical day, meetings and classes can reduce my workday by half, leaving little room for focused work. 

In summary, I urge you to approach shallow work with skepticism. Its detrimental effects are often underestimated, while its importance is overvalued. The strategies that follow will assist in acting on this understanding. 

### Schedule Every Minute of Your Day

If you’re between the ages of twenty-five and thirty-four and living in Britain, you likely watch more television than you realize. A survey revealed that this age group estimated they spent about fifteen to sixteen hours per week watching TV, but actual consumption was closer to twenty-eight hours.

This miscalculation of time usage isn’t unique to British television. Similar discrepancies exist across various groups. For instance, Americans believe they sleep an average of seven hours a night, but actual sleep averages 8.6 hours. Another study found that individuals claiming to work sixty to sixty-four hours per week were actually averaging around forty-four hours.

These examples illustrate a critical point: we often operate on autopilot, making it challenging to prevent trivial tasks from infiltrating our schedules. To confront this, I must cultivate the habit of pausing before taking action and asking myself, “What makes the most sense right now?” The strategy I’m about to outline is designed to compel me into these behaviors. It may seem extreme at first, but it will soon prove invaluable in my pursuit of maximizing the benefits of deep work: schedule every minute of my day.

### Daily Scheduling Strategy

At the start of each workday, I recommend turning to a fresh page in a dedicated notebook. On the left-hand side, I’ll mark every other line with an hour of the day, covering my typical work hours. Next, I’ll divide my workday into blocks and assign activities to each. For instance, I might allocate nine a.m. to eleven a.m. for writing a client’s press release, drawing a box that spans those lines and labeling it accordingly. I can also schedule time for lunch or relaxation breaks, ensuring that each block is at least thirty minutes long. This approach allows me to group similar tasks into broader blocks instead of creating a unique box for every minor task.

Once I finish scheduling, every minute will have a designated purpose, serving as my guide throughout the day. However, challenges may arise. My time estimates might be inaccurate, or interruptions could disrupt my carefully crafted schedule. If this happens, I should take a moment to revise my schedule for the remaining time. I can either turn to a new page or cross out blocks and create new ones to the right. My goal isn’t to adhere rigidly to a schedule but to maintain a thoughtful approach to time allocation, even if adjustments are necessary.

To introduce more stability, I can employ a few strategies. First, I should acknowledge that I will likely underestimate the time needed for tasks. Over time, I can strive for more accurate predictions. 

Second, I can use overflow conditional blocks. If I’m uncertain about the duration of an activity, I can block off the expected time and follow it with an additional block for overflow. 

Lastly, I should be generous with task blocks, making them longer than necessary to accommodate unexpected issues that arise during the day.

In addressing concerns about rigidity in scheduling, I emphasize that my approach encourages spontaneity. I allocate time for speculative thinking and discussions, allowing for unexpected insights to take precedence over my schedule when necessary. This method fosters thoughtfulness rather than constraint, helping me prioritize deep work while accommodating interruptions. 

Ultimately, I must consider what percentage of my time should be allocated to shallow work. For most knowledge workers, this typically falls between 30 to 50 percent. Sticking to this budget may require behavioral changes, such as declining overly shallow projects and creating space for substantial deep work.

### Establishing Support and Understanding Shallow Work

Starting this process with a conversation with my boss is vital because it establishes implicit support from my workplace. If I work for someone else, this agreement justifies my decision to decline obligations or restructure projects to minimize shallow work. I can explain that it’s necessary for me to meet my targeted mix of work types. As discussed in Chapter 2, shallow work proliferates in knowledge work because we often fail to see its cumulative impact on our schedules. We evaluate these tasks individually, where each appears reasonable and convenient. However, the tools I’ve outlined allow me to make this impact explicit. I can confidently present to my boss, “This is the exact percentage of my time spent last week on shallow work,” compelling them to approve that ratio.

Faced with these numbers and the economic implications they reveal—such as the inefficiency of paying a highly trained professional to spend thirty hours a week on emails and meetings—a boss is likely to conclude that I need to decline certain tasks and streamline others, even if it complicates life for them, for me, or for my colleagues. Ultimately, a business’s objective is to generate value, not to ensure that employees have an easy work life.

For those of us who are self-employed, this exercise forces a confrontation with the reality of how little time we actually spend producing value amidst our busy schedules. These hard numbers provide the confidence needed to begin scaling back on shallow activities that drain my time. Without this quantitative insight, it’s challenging for an entrepreneur to say no to opportunities that seem promising. I might convince myself that I must be active on Twitter or maintain a vibrant Facebook presence. Yet, when I consider these tasks in isolation, it becomes clear that saying no is essential for reclaiming my time and focusing on what truly matters.

### Managing Email Effectively

I serve as my own speaking agent for requests. If you wish to contact me, I provide a special-purpose email address with specific conditions and a reduced expectation of my response. If you have an offer, opportunity, or introduction that could make my life more interesting, please email me at interesting [at] calnewport.com. I will only respond to proposals that align well with my schedule and interests.

This method encourages my correspondents to self-filter before reaching out. Implementing this filter has significantly decreased the time I spend managing my inbox. Previously, I had a general-purpose email address, leading to an overwhelming influx of lengthy emails seeking advice. By using a sender filter, I have eliminated most of these communications, drastically reducing the number of messages in my writing inbox.

Resetting expectations is crucial. By stating that I will likely not respond, my inbox becomes a collection of opportunities I can review when I have time, allowing me to engage only with those that make sense for me. This shift can be liberating.

Initially, I worried that implementing a sender filter might come across as pretentious. However, most individuals readily accept the idea of controlling their incoming communication. People value clarity and generally do not expect a response if they do not anticipate one.

### Sender Filters

Although he started with a similar motivation as mine, Herbert’s filters took a different form. To contact him, you must first consult an FAQ to ensure your question hasn’t already been answered. If you pass through this FAQ filter, you then fill out a survey that helps him further screen inquiries relevant to his expertise. For those who make it past this step, Herbert enforces a small fee before communication can occur. This fee isn’t about generating extra income; rather, it serves to filter for individuals who are serious about receiving and acting on advice. His filters allow him to assist people and encounter interesting opportunities while reducing incoming communication to a manageable level.

Another example is Antonio Centeno, who runs the popular Real Man Style blog. Centeno’s sender filter involves a two-step process. If you have a question, he directs you to a public forum to post it, believing it’s inefficient to answer the same questions repeatedly in private conversations. If you make it past this initial step, you must commit to three promises by checking boxes:

1. I am not asking Antonio a style question I could find by searching Google for 10 minutes.
2. I am not SPAMMING Antonio with a generic request to promote my unrelated business.
3. I will do a good deed for a random stranger if Antonio responds within 23 hours.

The message box for your inquiry doesn’t appear on the contact page until you’ve checked all three boxes.

In summary, while the technologies underlying email are transformative, the current social conventions guiding their use are underdeveloped. The idea that all messages, regardless of purpose or sender, arrive in the same undifferentiated inbox, with an expectation of a timely response, is absurdly unproductive. The sender filter represents a small but valuable step toward a more efficient communication landscape—an idea whose time has come, especially for the growing number of entrepreneurs and freelancers who receive substantial incoming communication and can dictate their accessibility. I would also love to see similar principles applied to intra-office communication in large organizations, but as I argued in Chapter 2, we are likely a long way from that reality. If you have the opportunity, consider implementing sender filters to reclaim control over your time and attention.

### Do More Work When You Send or Reply to Emails

Consider the following standard emails:

Email #1: “It was great to meet you last week. I’d love to follow up on some of those issues we discussed. Do you want to grab coffee?”

Email #2: “We should get back to the research problem we discussed during my last visit. Remind me where we are with that?”

Email #3: “I took a stab at that article we discussed. It’s attached. Thoughts?”

These examples represent many of the messages that fill knowledge workers' inboxes and pose potential productivity pitfalls. How you respond will significantly impact the time and attention the ensuing conversation consumes.

Interrogative emails often trigger an instinct to quickly respond with the simplest answer that clears the message from your inbox—at least temporarily. A quick reply provides minor relief by shifting the responsibility implied by the message back to the sender. However, this relief is short-lived, as the responsibility will continue to bounce back, draining your time and attention. Therefore, I suggest that when faced with such questions, you pause and consider the following key prompt:

What is the project represented by this message, and what is the most efficient process for bringing this project to a successful conclusion?

Once you’ve answered this question, replace a hasty response with one that outlines the process you identified, clarifies the current step, and emphasizes the next step. I call this the process-centric approach to email, designed to minimize both the number of emails you receive and the mental clutter they generate.

To illustrate this process, consider the following process-centric responses to the earlier sample emails:

Process-Centric Response to Email #1: “I’d love to grab coffee. Let’s meet at the Starbucks on campus. Below, I’ve listed two days next week when I’m free, along with three times for each day. If any of these combinations work for you, let me know, and I’ll consider your reply confirmation for the meeting. If none of these options suit you, feel free to call me at the number below, and we can work out a time. Looking forward to it.”

Process-Centric Response to Email #2: “I agree that we should revisit this problem. Here’s what I suggest: 

Sometime in the next week, please email me everything you remember about our discussion on the problem. Once I receive that, I’ll create a shared directory for the project and add a document summarizing what you sent, along with my own recollections. In that document, I’ll highlight the two or three most promising next steps.

We can then tackle those next steps over the next few weeks and check back in. I propose we schedule a phone call for a month from now to discuss this further. Below, I’ve listed some dates and times when I’m available for a call. When you respond with your notes, please indicate which date and time works best for you, and we’ll consider that reply confirmation for the call. I look forward to delving into this problem.”

Process-Centric Response to Email #3: “Thanks for sending that draft of the article. I’ll read it and return an edited version with comments by Friday (the 10th). In my version, I’ll edit what I can and add comments to highlight areas where I think you’re better suited to make improvements. At that point, you should have what you need to polish and submit the final draft, so no need to reply to this message or follow up with me after I send the edits—unless there’s an issue.”

In crafting these sample responses, I identified the project implied by the message. The term “project” is used loosely here; it can encompass large, obvious projects, such as making progress on a research problem, as well as smaller logistical challenges like setting up a coffee meeting. I then thought through a process that would lead us from the current state to a desired outcome with minimal messages exchanged. The final step was to write a reply that clearly outlines this process and where we currently stand. While these examples focus on email replies, a similar approach applies when composing a new email from scratch.

The process-centric approach to email can significantly mitigate the negative impact of this technology on your time and attention for two reasons. First, it reduces the number of emails in your inbox—sometimes dramatically. A simple task like scheduling a coffee meeting can easily spiral into half a dozen or more messages over several days if you’re not careful with your replies. This reduction in emails leads to less time spent in your inbox and less mental energy expended when you do check it.

Second, borrowing terminology from David Allen, a well-crafted process-centric message immediately “closes the loop” regarding the project at hand. When a project is initiated by an email you send or receive, it occupies mental space—becoming something that’s “on your plate” that needs addressing. This method closes that open loop as soon as it forms. By working through the entire process, adding relevant commitments to your task lists and calendar, and bringing the other party up to speed, your mind can reclaim the mental space the project once occupied. Less mental clutter means more cognitive resources available for deep thinking.

Initially, process-centric emails might feel unnatural. They require you to invest more time thinking about your messages before composing them. In the moment, this may seem like you’re spending more time on email. However, it’s essential to remember that the extra two to three minutes spent now will save you many more minutes later by preventing unnecessary back-and-forth exchanges.

Another concern is that process-centric messages can come across as stilted or overly technical. Current social conventions surrounding email often promote a conversational tone that clashes with the systematic structure of process-centric communication. If this worries you, consider adding a longer conversational introduction to your messages. You can even separate the process-centric portion from the conversational opening with a divider line or label it “Proposed Next Steps,” making its technical tone feel more appropriate in context.

Ultimately, these minor inconveniences are worthwhile. By investing more thought upfront into what’s genuinely being proposed by the emails that come and go in your inbox, you’ll significantly reduce the negative impact of this technology on your ability to engage in meaningful work.

### Professorial Email Sorting

To help you navigate email communication more effectively, I propose the following guidelines for determining which messages warrant a response:

- Do not reply if the email is ambiguous or makes it difficult for you to formulate a reasonable response.
  
- Do not reply if the email does not contain a question or proposal that piques your interest.
  
- Do not reply if responding would yield no significant benefit and not responding would not result in negative consequences.

While there are exceptions—such as when an ambiguous message comes from a high-ranking official—this approach encourages you to be more discerning about when to click "reply."

Initially, this may feel uncomfortable, as it challenges the convention that replies are mandatory regardless of the message's relevance. Some may feel confused or upset by this shift, but that’s okay. As Tim Ferriss noted, “Develop the habit of letting small bad things happen. If you don’t, you’ll never find time for the life-changing big things.” People will quickly adjust their expectations to fit your communication style, and your lack of response to hastily written messages is unlikely to be a pivotal event in their lives.

Once you embrace this approach, you will begin to reap its rewards. Two common sayings about email overload highlight the issues at hand: sending emails generates more emails, and dealing with ambiguous or irrelevant emails is a significant source of inbox-related stress. By adopting the proposed guidelines, you will send fewer emails and ignore those that are not straightforward, significantly reducing the hold your inbox has over your time and attention.

### The Power of Deep Work

The story of Microsoft’s founding is legendary. In 1974, Bill Gates, a young Harvard student, spotted the Altair, the first personal computer, on the cover of Popular Electronics. Recognizing an opportunity, Gates, with Paul Allen and Monte Davidoff, spent eight weeks developing a version of the BASIC programming language for the Altair. This narrative exemplifies Gates’s insight and boldness, but it also highlights his extraordinary ability to engage in deep work.

Walter Isaacson noted that Gates worked with such intensity during this period that he would often fall asleep at his keyboard, only to wake up and seamlessly continue coding. This ability to focus set Gates apart from others, as he was a "serial obsessor."

In our rapidly evolving information age, it’s easy to succumb to distractions. Some lament the attention people devote to their phones, while others celebrate increased connectivity. However, my commitment to deep work is a pragmatic acknowledgment that concentration is a skill that facilitates valuable tasks. Deep work is significant not because distraction is inherently evil, but because it empowered Gates to launch a billion-dollar industry in less than a semester.

### My Journey with Deep Work

Throughout my career, I have repeatedly relearned the importance of deep work. During graduate school, prioritizing this skill allowed me to produce quality peer-reviewed papers while maintaining a balanced schedule. However, as I transitioned to a professorship, I felt anxious about integrating deep work into a more demanding schedule.

To prepare, I imposed artificial constraints on my time, simulating the limited free time I anticipated as a professor. I began taking extended lunch breaks and signed a contract to write my fourth book, *So Good They Can’t Ignore You*. These constraints honed my ability to work deeply.

After accepting a position as a computer science professor at Georgetown University, my obligations increased significantly. Yet, I maintained and even improved my research productivity, doubling my output to four quality papers per year.

During my third year as a professor, I recommitted to deep work, driven by the need to write this book and prepare for tenure. I became ruthless in declining time-consuming commitments and worked in isolated locations. This renewed focus allowed me to publish nine peer-reviewed papers while adhering to my rule against evening work.

This experience reinforced the power of deep work. It enabled me to double my academic productivity in a year filled with personal and professional challenges. Embracing depth fosters a life rich in productivity and significance. If you are willing to set aside distractions and fears, you will discover the transformative potential of deep work.
