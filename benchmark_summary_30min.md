### Benn's Journey to Deep Work

Benn is an intelligent individual who graduated from the University of Virginia with a degree in economics. Like many graduates, he had career aspirations, but he soon realized that his ambitions were limited by his skills, which could be reduced to an Excel macro. Determined to enhance his value, Benn announced to his family that he would leave his job and transition into computer programming. The challenge? He had no coding skills.

As a computer scientist, I can attest that programming is challenging. Most new developers spend four years in college learning the basics before landing their first job, and competition for top positions is fierce. Benn didn’t have that luxury. After quitting his job at a financial firm, he moved back home to prepare for his next step. His parents were pleased he had a plan, but they were less enthusiastic about a long-term arrangement. Benn needed to acquire a hard skill quickly.

This is where Benn faced a common issue that hinders many knowledge workers from advancing in their careers. Learning complex subjects like programming requires intense focus—deep work. Unfortunately, most knowledge workers, including Benn, struggle with this.

“I was always getting on the Internet and checking my e-mail; I couldn’t stop myself,” Benn reflected on his mindset before leaving finance. He shared a story about a project where he was tasked with writing a business plan. Lacking experience, he read five existing plans for insights but struggled to focus. He admits he spent “98 percent of my time” browsing the Web, causing the project to fall by the wayside.

Recognizing his challenges with deep work, Benn committed to learning how to code while training his mind to focus. His method was drastic: “I locked myself in a room with no computer—just textbooks, notecards, and a highlighter.” Initially difficult, these distraction-free periods became effective. Over time, he improved his concentration, eventually spending five or more uninterrupted hours each day learning.

After two months of intensive study, Benn enrolled in the rigorous Dev Bootcamp, a hundred-hour-a-week crash course in web application programming. Thanks to his preparation and newfound ability to engage in deep work, he thrived, graduating as the top student in his class.

Benn quickly secured a developer position at a San Francisco tech start-up, earning $100,000—an impressive leap from his previous salary of $40,000. Now a staunch advocate of deep work, he structured his life to prioritize focus, arriving early at the office to maximize his productivity.

Benn’s journey illustrates a vital lesson: deep work is not merely a nostalgic notion but a valuable skill in today’s economy.

### The Metric Black Hole

In the fall of 2012, Tom Cochran, the chief technology officer of Atlantic Media, became increasingly concerned about the time he spent on e-mail. To quantify this unease, he tracked his behavior and found that in one week, he received 511 e-mails and sent 284, averaging about 160 e-mails per day. Even if he spent only thirty seconds per message, he was dedicating nearly an hour and a half daily to managing information.

Cochran then considered the broader implications for his company. How much time were Atlantic Media employees spending managing information instead of focusing on their specialized tasks? To find out, he gathered company-wide statistics on e-mails sent per day and average word count per e-mail. Combining these figures with employees’ typing speed, reading speed, and salary revealed staggering results: Atlantic Media was spending over a million dollars annually on e-mail processing, with each message costing the company around ninety-five cents in labor.

Cochran summarized this finding by stating that a “free and frictionless” method of communication had soft costs equivalent to procuring a small company Learjet. This realization prompted him to reflect on the inefficiencies of e-mail communication and its impact on productivity, highlighting the need for a more focused approach to work that prioritizes deep engagement over constant connectivity.

### The Complexity of Measuring Deep Work

Cochran’s experiment revealed that assessing the impact of our email habits on the bottom line is complex. He conducted a company-wide survey, gathered IT statistics, compiled salary data, and analyzed typing and reading speeds, all while running the data through a statistical model. Ultimately, he found it challenging to isolate the value generated by frequent email use against its costs.

This complexity extends to many behaviors that either hinder or enhance deep work. While we recognize that distractions have costs, measuring these impacts is difficult. French economist Thomas Piketty highlighted this in his study of rising executive salaries, noting that it’s hard to measure individual contributions to a firm's output. Without clear metrics, irrational outcomes, like excessive executive salaries, can arise.

The concept of the "metric black hole" illustrates why the bottom-line impact of behaviors undermining deep work is often obscured. Many behaviors thrive despite being hard to quantify, leading to unstable business practices that detract from deep work.

### The Principle of Least Resistance

In today’s culture of connectivity, professionals feel compelled to respond to emails promptly. Leslie Perlow's research at the Boston Consulting Group showed that this constant connectivity may harm productivity and well-being. Yet, organizations persist in this behavior because it’s easier.

The Principle of Least Resistance explains this tendency: when feedback on behaviors is unclear, we gravitate toward the simplest actions. Quick responses to emails simplify daily tasks, while regular meetings provide a false sense of progress. In the absence of metrics, individuals often default to what feels easiest, undermining depth and potentially harming the bottom line.

### The Principle of Least Resistance

Consider the frustratingly common practice of forwarding an email to colleagues with a vague prompt, such as, “Thoughts?” These emails take seconds to compose but can demand significant time from recipients as they work to formulate a coherent response. From the sender’s perspective, they’re easier—a way to clear something from their inbox with minimal effort.

The Principle of Least Resistance, shielded by the metric black hole, supports work cultures that spare us from the discomfort of concentration and planning, but at the cost of long-term satisfaction and real value creation. This principle drives us toward shallow work in an economy that increasingly rewards depth.

### The Challenge of Deep Work

Participants in a study revealed that their most common desires included eating, sleeping, and sex, alongside distractions like checking email, social networking, and watching television. Your willpower resembles a muscle that tires with exertion. This explains why participants struggled to resist distractions; over time, their limited willpower was drained. The same will happen to you unless you adopt strategic habits.

The key to developing a deep work habit is to move beyond good intentions and establish routines that minimize the willpower needed to maintain uninterrupted concentration. For instance, if you suddenly decide to switch focus to a demanding task amidst distractions, you will draw heavily from your willpower, making success unlikely. In contrast, setting specific times and quiet locations for deep tasks will require less willpower to initiate and sustain focus.

### Decide on Your Depth Philosophy

Establishing your own philosophy for integrating deep work into your professional life is crucial. Attempting to schedule deep work ad hoc is ineffective. You must choose a philosophy that aligns with your circumstances, as a mismatch can derail your deep work habit.

The monastic philosophy, exemplified by Donald Knuth, seeks to maximize deep efforts by eliminating shallow obligations. Knuth, who has avoided email since 1990, prioritizes uninterrupted concentration for his work. Similarly, Neal Stephenson, a science fiction writer, minimizes distractions to produce high-quality novels.

In contrast, the bimodal philosophy, illustrated by Carl Jung, divides time into stretches dedicated to deep work while leaving the rest open for other activities. Jung's retreats allowed him to focus intensely, while his life in Zurich was filled with distractions. Adam Grant, a Wharton professor, exemplifies this approach by concentrating his courses into one semester to dedicate the other to deep work.

Embracing the bimodal philosophy allows for a balance between deep and shallow work, recognizing the value of both in professional life. While implementing this philosophy may seem daunting, it is often more compatible with various job types than one might assume.

### The Rhythmic Philosophy of Deep Work Scheduling

Earlier, I referenced a study by Harvard Business School professor Leslie Perlow. In this study, management consultants were asked to disconnect for a full day each workweek. Initially, they feared negative client reactions, but clients were indifferent. As Jung, Grant, and Perlow’s subjects discovered, people generally respect your right to become inaccessible if these periods are clearly defined and well communicated, and outside of these stretches, you remain readily available.

In the early days of the *Seinfeld* show, Jerry Seinfeld advised aspiring comic Brad Isaac to write every day. Seinfeld maintained a calendar on his wall, crossing out each day he wrote with a big red X. “Just keep at it, and the chain will grow longer every day. Your only job next is to not break the chain.” This chain method exemplifies the rhythmic philosophy of deep work scheduling, transforming deep work into a regular habit.

To implement this philosophy, consider the following:

- **Where you’ll work:** Choose a space that minimizes distractions, signaling to your brain that it’s time to focus.

- **When you’ll work:** Establish a consistent time for deep work to condition your mind for concentration. 

- **How you’ll prepare:** Create rituals that signal the start of deep work, such as setting up your workspace or engaging in a brief mindfulness exercise.

- **What tools you’ll use:** Decide in advance what resources you’ll need, reducing the temptation to stray into shallow work.

- **How you’ll reward yourself:** Incorporate a reward system to reinforce your deep work habits, making the process more enjoyable.

By establishing these rituals, you create a structured environment that fosters deep work. The predictability of your routine minimizes the friction often associated with transitioning into deep concentration.

We can reject the depth-destroying open office concept without dismissing the innovation-producing theory of serendipitous creativity. The key lies in a hub-and-spoke arrangement: regularly expose yourself to new ideas in hubs while maintaining a spoke for deep, focused work.

Distraction is a significant barrier to depth, so separate your pursuit of serendipitous encounters from deep thinking. Leverage the whiteboard effect whenever feasible, as collaboration can propel you toward deeper insights.

### Understanding Shallow Work

While the value of deep work far surpasses that of shallow work, it doesn’t necessitate a quixotic pursuit of a schedule where every moment is devoted to depth. A certain amount of shallow work is necessary to sustain most knowledge work roles. I might avoid checking my email every ten minutes, but I won’t last long if I ignore important messages altogether. Thus, the goal is to manage shallow work rather than eliminate it entirely.

Cognitive capacity also plays a role. Deep work can be draining, and performance psychologists have found that individuals can sustain such efforts for limited periods—about one hour for novices and up to four hours for those more experienced. Once I reach my deep work limit, pushing beyond that will yield diminishing returns. Shallow work becomes problematic when it encroaches upon my limited deep work capacity.

As a professor, my schedule is traditionally less burdened by commitments, yet they still carve out significant portions of my time. Reflecting on a random day from last semester, I note that meetings and classes already reduce my eight-hour workday by four hours. Even if I manage to cram all remaining shallow tasks into a half-hour block, I would still fall short of achieving four hours of deep work. This reality underscores the urgency of minimizing shallow work, as the typical knowledge workday is often more fragmented than many realize.

### Scheduling Every Minute of Your Day

To combat this fragmentation, I will schedule every minute of my day. At the start of each workday, I’ll turn to a fresh page in a dedicated notebook. On the left side, I’ll mark every other line with an hour of the day. I’ll divide my work hours into blocks and assign specific activities to each block. For instance, I might allocate nine a.m. to eleven a.m. for writing a client’s press release. 

Once I finish scheduling, every minute will have a designated purpose. However, disruptions are inevitable. If my schedule is disrupted, I should take a moment to create a revised schedule for the remaining time. I can turn to a new page, erase and redraw blocks, or cross out the remaining blocks and create new ones. 

If frequent revisions become overwhelming, I can employ a few strategies for stability. First, I should acknowledge that I will likely underestimate the time required for most tasks. Over time, I should strive to make more accurate predictions regarding task durations. 

Second, I can use overflow conditional blocks. If I’m uncertain about how long a task might take, I can block off the expected time and follow it with an additional block that serves a dual purpose. This approach accommodates unpredictability without necessitating constant changes to my written schedule. 

Lastly, I should be generous with my use of task blocks, incorporating many throughout my day and making them longer than necessary to accommodate unexpected events. 

### Encouraging Flexibility

In my experience advocating for daily scheduling, I’ve encountered concerns that this level of planning may become overly restrictive. However, I actively encourage spontaneity in my schedule. If I stumble upon an important insight, it’s a valid reason to disregard the rest of my schedule for the day (except for non-negotiable commitments). 

This type of scheduling is not about constraint; it’s about thoughtfulness. It empowers me to take control of my time and prioritize what truly matters. By quantifying the time spent on shallow activities, I can gain clarity on where my efforts are valuable. 

This process compels me to pause during my day and ask, “What makes sense for me to do with the time that remains?” I would argue that someone who combines thorough scheduling with the flexibility to adapt their plans will likely uncover more creative insights than someone who leaves their day open and unstructured. 

In essence, cultivating a deep work habit requires me to treat my time with respect. A solid first step is to decide in advance how I will spend every minute of my workday. Initially, I may resist this notion, but I must overcome this skepticism toward structure to unlock my true potential as a creator of meaningful work. 

### Quantifying the Depth of Every Activity

One significant advantage of scheduling my day is the ability to assess how much time I’m dedicating to shallow activities. However, extracting this insight can be challenging, as it’s not always clear how to categorize a given task as shallow or deep. 

Some activities clearly fit the definition of shallow work: noncognitively demanding, logistical tasks performed while distracted. For instance, checking emails or scheduling a conference call is undoubtedly shallow. However, other tasks can be more ambiguous. 

Consider these examples:

- Editing a draft of an academic article that I and a collaborator plan to submit to a journal.
- Creating a PowerPoint presentation about this quarter’s sales figures.
- Attending a meeting to discuss the current status of an important project and agree on the next steps.

At first glance, it’s not obvious how to categorize tasks. The first two involve considerable demands, while the last seems crucial for advancing a key work objective. This strategy aims to provide a reliable metric for resolving such ambiguity, allowing me to make clear and consistent decisions about where specific tasks fall on the shallow-to-deep spectrum. To achieve this, I evaluate activities by asking a simple yet revealing question:

### How long would it take (in months) to train a smart recent college graduate with no specialized training in my field to complete this task?

Let’s apply this question to our ambiguous tasks:

### Analyzing Example #1

Properly editing an academic paper requires understanding the nuances of the work itself and the broader literature to ensure accurate descriptions and citations. This task demands cutting-edge knowledge of an academic field, which typically takes years of dedicated study at the graduate level and beyond. Thus, the answer to our question would likely be substantial—perhaps fifty to seventy-five months.

### Analyzing Example #2

Creating a PowerPoint presentation about quarterly sales involves three components: knowledge of how to use PowerPoint, familiarity with the standard format for these presentations within my organization, and an understanding of the sales metrics tracked and how to translate them into appropriate graphs. A recent college graduate would likely already know how to use PowerPoint, and learning the standard format shouldn’t take more than a week. The real challenge lies in understanding the metrics and how to present them effectively, which might require an additional month of training. Therefore, I can conservatively estimate this task at two months.

### Analyzing Example #3

Meetings can be tricky to assess. They may seem tedious but are often framed as essential to key organizational activities. How long would it take to train a bright recent college graduate to take my place in a planning meeting? They would need to understand the project well enough to know its milestones and the skills of its participants. While they might not need deep expertise in the project topic, they would require some insight into interpersonal dynamics and how projects are executed within the organization. A bright recent graduate could likely step in after three months of learning the ropes, so we’ll use three months as our answer.

This question serves as a thought experiment; I’m not suggesting that I actually hire a recent college graduate to take over low-scoring tasks. However, the answers it yields help me objectively quantify the shallowness or depth of various activities. If a task requires many months of training to replicate, it indicates that the task leverages hard-won expertise. As I argued earlier, tasks that utilize my expertise tend to be deep and offer a dual benefit: they provide greater value for the time invested and stretch my abilities, leading to improvement. Conversely, tasks that a recent college graduate can quickly grasp are shallow.

### What Should I Do with This Strategy?

Once I understand where my activities fall on the deep-to-shallow scale, I should bias my time toward the former. Reassessing our case studies, for instance, I see that the first task is one I should prioritize as a valuable use of time, while the second and third tasks are activities that should be minimized. They may feel productive, but their return on investment is minimal.

Of course, shifting away from shallow work toward deeper tasks isn’t always straightforward, even after accurately labeling my commitments. This leads us to the following strategies, which will provide specific guidance on how to achieve this challenging goal.

### Ask Your Boss for a Shallow Work Budget

An important question that often goes unasked is: What percentage of my time should be allocated to shallow work? This strategy encourages me to pose this question. If I have a boss, I should initiate a conversation about it. (I’ll likely need to define what “shallow” and “deep” work means first.) If I’m self-employed, I should reflect on this question myself. In both scenarios, I should arrive at a specific answer and, importantly, strive to adhere to this budget. The preceding and following strategies will assist me in achieving this goal.

For most individuals in non-entry-level knowledge work roles, the answer will typically fall within the 30 to 50 percent range. There’s a psychological aversion to the idea of spending the majority of my time on unskilled tasks, making 50 percent a natural upper limit. Simultaneously, most bosses will become concerned if this percentage dips below 30 percent, fearing that I might become a knowledge work hermit who contemplates grand ideas but neglects to respond to emails.

Adhering to this budget will likely necessitate behavioral changes. I will probably find myself needing to decline projects that seem shallow while also actively reducing the shallowness of my existing commitments. This budget might prompt me to eliminate a weekly status meeting in favor of results-driven reporting (“Let me know when you’ve made significant progress; then we’ll talk”). It may also lead me to spend more mornings in communication isolation or to reconsider the importance of responding promptly and thoroughly to every cc’d email that lands in my inbox.

These changes are beneficial for my goal of making deep work central to my professional life. On one hand, they don’t require me to abandon my core shallow obligations—an approach that would create problems and resentment—since I will still be dedicating a significant amount of time to such efforts. On the other hand, they compel me to impose a hard limit on the less urgent obligations that can insidiously infiltrate my schedule. This limit creates space for substantial deep work on a consistent basis.

The reason these decisions should begin with a conversation with my boss is that this agreement establishes implicit support from my workplace. If I work for someone else, this strategy provides justification when I decline an obligation or restructure a project to minimize shallow work. I can explain that it’s necessary for me to meet my prescribed target mix of work types. As I discussed in Chapter 2, one reason shallow work persists in abundance within knowledge work is that we rarely perceive the total impact of such efforts on our schedules. Instead, we tend to evaluate these behaviors individually in the moment, where each task can seem reasonable and convenient. However, the tools from earlier in this rule allow me to make this impact explicit. I can confidently tell my boss, “This is the exact percentage of my time spent last week on shallow work,” compelling them to give explicit approval for that ratio. Faced with these numbers and the economic reality they reveal (for instance, it’s incredibly wasteful to pay a highly trained professional to send emails and attend meetings for thirty hours a week), a boss will naturally conclude that I need to decline certain tasks and streamline others—even if this makes life less convenient for them, for me, or for my coworkers. Ultimately, a business’s goal is to generate value, not to ensure that its employees’ lives are as easy as possible.

If I’m self-employed, this exercise forces me to confront the reality of how little time I’m actually producing value in my “busy” schedule. These hard numbers will provide the confidence I need to start scaling back on the shallow activities that drain my time. Without these figures, it’s challenging for an entrepreneur to say no to any opportunity that might yield some positive return. I might convince myself, “I have to be on Twitter! I must maintain an active Facebook presence! I need to tweak the widgets on my blog!” because, when considered in isolation, saying no to these tasks feels overwhelming.

### Additional Strategies for Managing Shallow Work

Tip #1: Make Senders Do More Work

I can create an automated response for scheduling inquiries. This approach shifts the burden of communication onto the sender, requiring them to think more critically about their request. By doing this, I reduce the volume of shallow interactions that demand my attention, allowing me to focus on deeper work.

Tip #2: Schedule Time for E-mail

I allocate specific times in my day to check and respond to e-mails. This practice prevents e-mail from intruding on my deep work sessions. By designating a set period for e-mail, I can manage my time more effectively and ensure that I’m not constantly distracted by incoming messages. This structured approach allows me to maintain focus on my most important tasks while still addressing necessary communications.

Tip #3: Use E-mail Filters and Folders

Another effective strategy is to utilize e-mail filters and folders to organize incoming messages. By categorizing e-mails based on their urgency and relevance, I can prioritize my responses and minimize the clutter in my inbox. This system helps me quickly identify which messages require immediate attention and which can wait, further protecting my time for deep work.

In summary, while e-mail is an unavoidable aspect of modern work life, I have found ways to regain control over it. By making senders do more work, scheduling specific times for e-mail, and using filters to organize my inbox, I can significantly reduce the impact of shallow work on my productivity. These strategies not only help me maintain focus on my deep work but also empower me to take charge of my professional life in a way that aligns with my goals.

I encountered a somewhat rare approach to email among certain individuals: their default behavior upon receiving an email message is to refrain from responding. Over time, I came to understand the philosophy behind this behavior. They believed that when it comes to email, it is the sender's responsibility to persuade the receiver that a reply is worthwhile. If the sender fails to make a convincing case and does not sufficiently minimize the effort required for the professor to respond, they simply do not receive a reply.

For instance, consider the following email, which would likely not elicit a response from many of the prominent figures at the Institute:

### Email Communication

Hi professor. I’d love to stop by sometime to talk about <topic X>. Are you available?

This message is vague and requires too much effort to respond to. In contrast, here’s a revised version that is more likely to generate a response:

Hi professor. I’m working on a project similar to <topic X> with my advisor, <professor Y>. Would it be alright if I stopped by during the last fifteen minutes of your office hours on Thursday to explain what we’re doing in more detail and see if it might complement your current project?

This version clearly articulates the value of the meeting and minimizes the effort required from the recipient.

I encourage you to adopt a discerning approach to email. Consider applying the following rules to determine which messages warrant a response:

### Professorial Email Sorting

Do not reply to an email if any of the following applies:

- It is ambiguous or makes it difficult for you to formulate a reasonable response.
- It is not a question or proposal that interests you.
- Responding would not yield any significant benefit, and not responding would not result in any negative consequences.

While there are exceptions—such as messages from your company’s CEO—this approach encourages you to be more selective about when to click “reply.”

Initially, this may feel uncomfortable, as it challenges the expectation that replies are obligatory. Some may feel confused or upset, but that’s okay. As Tim Ferriss wrote, “Develop the habit of letting small bad things happen.” People quickly adjust their expectations based on your communication habits.

Once you move past the discomfort, you will reap the rewards. Sending fewer emails and ignoring ambiguous ones will significantly reduce the hold your inbox has over your time and attention.
